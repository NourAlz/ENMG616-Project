\section{Introduction}
% ------------------------------------

\subsection{Problem Overview}

An online retailer wants to show each customer ten products that match their
interests. We build a recommendation model that learns customer preferences
and product representations from real reviews and product descriptions. Given
a customer's observed history, the model scores every candidate product and
returns the ten with the highest scores.

The model is a hybrid: each product is represented both by a vector learned
from interactions and by a fixed text vector computed from its title and
description. The text part lets the model score a \emph{cold} product, one
that has a description but no training interactions, which a purely
interaction-based model cannot do.

The question we study is not which model is best but how the training
algorithm affects the recommendations. The same model, objective, data and
starting point are trained with four methods: mini-batch stochastic gradient
descent (SGD), sharpness-aware minimization (SAM)~\cite{sam}, SGD with a
sharpness-aware learning rate (SALR)~\cite{salr}, and block coordinate descent
(BCD).

\subsection{Project Objectives}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Formulate the hybrid model and its regularized pairwise objective,
    derive its gradients, and characterize its convexity.
    \item Implement SGD, SAM, SALR and BCD on a common model interface, with
    all gradient work counted in the same unit.
    \item Select one regularization pair shared by all methods, tune each
    method, and select checkpoints using ordinary-validation results only.
    \item Compare the methods on recommendation quality for held-out
    interactions, on optimization progress, on variability across three
    seeds, and on computational cost.
    \item Evaluate the same fitted models, without retraining, on products
    that have no training interactions.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Dataset Description}
\label{sec:data}
% ------------------------------------

The data are an instructor-prepared subset of the Amazon Reviews 2023
dataset~\cite{amazon2023}, drawn from the Sports and Outdoors category and
focused on outdoor recreation equipment. They contain reviews and product
descriptions for 170 customers and 286 products. We use the supplied features,
splits and triplets unchanged.

\paragraph{Ratings.} A rating is an integer from 1 to 5. A rating of 4 or 5 is
a positive interaction. Ratings of 1 to 3 remain part of the customer's
observed history but are not positive examples, and an unreviewed product is
unknown, not a confirmed dislike. Of the 1{,}897 training reviews, 1{,}812 are
positive.

\paragraph{Text features.} Each product $i$ has a fixed vector
$z_i \in \R^{384}$ with unit Euclidean norm, encoding its title and
description with the all-MiniLM-L6-v2 sentence encoder~\cite{minilm}. These
vectors are inputs: the encoder is neither trained nor modified. Sixty-three
products have a title but an empty description; their vectors are supplied
like all others.

\paragraph{Product groups.} The catalog is split into three disjoint groups
(Table~\ref{tab:catalogs}). Cold products appear in no training review and in
no training comparison.

\paragraph{Splits.} Training reviews define the customer histories and contain
only ordinary products; every customer has between 8 and 31 of them.
Validation reviews are used to choose hyperparameters and checkpoints, and
test reviews only to assess the selected models
(Table~\ref{tab:splits}).

\paragraph{Training comparisons.} A triplet $\tau = (u, i, j)$ contains a
customer $u$, an ordinary product $i$ that $u$ rated positively in training,
and an ordinary product $j$ that $u$ did not review in training. The supplied
set $\mathcal{T}$ has $N = 5{,}436$ triplets: three comparisons with distinct
unreviewed products for each of the 1{,}812 positive training reviews. A
further 121 held-out triplets are used only to report validation loss.

\paragraph{Evaluation customers.} Relevant products are those a customer rated
4 or 5 in the split being evaluated. The supplied relevant sets cover 121
validation and 110 test customers for ordinary recommendation, and 119
validation and 114 test customers for cold-start recommendation.

\paragraph{Loading.} Our loader reads the eight supplied files without
altering them and checks them against the rules above before any training:
identifier ranges, unit-norm text vectors, the three-way catalog split, the
absence of cold products from training, and the validity of every triplet and
relevant set. A file that violates a rule stops the run instead of having rows
dropped.

\section{Mathematical Formulation}
\label{sec:math}
% ------------------------------------

\subsection{Recommendation Model}

For customer $u$ and product $i$, the product representation $v_i$ and the
recommendation score $s_{ui}$ are
\begin{equation}
    v_i = q_i + W z_i, \qquad
    s_{ui} = b_i + p_u^\top v_i = b_i + p_u^\top (q_i + W z_i).
    \label{eq:score}
\end{equation}
Here $p_u \in \R^k$ is the customer's preference vector, $q_i \in \R^k$ is
product-specific information learned from interactions, $b_i \in \R$ is a
product bias shared across customers, $z_i \in \R^{384}$ is the fixed text
vector, and $W \in \R^{k \times 384}$ maps text features into the preference
space. We use $k = 32$ throughout. A higher score places the product higher in
that customer's ranking; the score is neither a predicted rating nor a
probability.

Let $P \in \R^{170 \times k}$ and $Q \in \R^{206 \times k}$ hold the customer
and ordinary-product vectors as rows and $b \in \R^{206}$ the biases. The
trainable parameters are $\theta = (P, Q, W, b)$, in total
$170 \cdot 32 + 206 \cdot 32 + 32 \cdot 384 + 206 = 24{,}526$ values.

A cold product has no learned vector or bias. We set $q_i = 0$ and $b_i = 0$,
so that
\begin{equation}
    s_{ui}^{\mathrm{cold}} = p_u^\top W z_i .
    \label{eq:cold}
\end{equation}
No untrained parameters are allocated for cold products, and there is no
customer-only bias, since it would cancel when comparing two products for the
same customer.

\subsection{Objective Function}

For a triplet $\tau = (u, i, j)$ the intended ordering is $s_{ui} > s_{uj}$.
With the score difference $\Delta_{uij}$, the pairwise logistic
loss~\cite{bpr} is
\begin{equation}
    \Delta_{uij} = s_{ui} - s_{uj}, \qquad
    \ell_{uij}(\theta) = \log\bigl(1 + \exp(-\Delta_{uij})\bigr).
    \label{eq:loss}
\end{equation}
The loss decreases as the positive product moves above its comparison product,
equals $\log 2$ at a tie, and grows linearly when the order is reversed.

For a mini-batch $B \subseteq \mathcal{T}$ let
$L_B(\theta) = |B|^{-1} \sum_{\tau \in B} \ell_\tau(\theta)$, and define
$L_{\mathcal{T}}$ in the same way over all training triplets. The optimization
problem is
\begin{equation}
    \min_{P, Q, W, b} \; F(\theta) = L_{\mathcal{T}}(\theta)
    + \frac{\lambda_f}{2}\bigl(\norm{P}_F^2 + \norm{Q}_F^2 + \norm{b}_2^2\bigr)
    + \frac{\lambda_w}{2}\norm{W}_F^2,
    \qquad \lambda_f, \lambda_w > 0 .
    \label{eq:objective}
\end{equation}
The first term rewards correct rankings. The two penalties discourage large
parameters that may overfit the training comparisons, with separate strengths
for the customer and product parameters and for the text transformation. We
write $F_B$ for the same expression with $L_B$ in place of $L_{\mathcal{T}}$.

\subsection{Gradient Derivations}

Let $\sigma(x) = 1/(1 + e^{-x})$. Differentiating \eqref{eq:loss} with respect
to the score difference gives
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\partial \ell_{uij}}{\partial \Delta_{uij}}
    = -\frac{e^{-\Delta_{uij}}}{1 + e^{-\Delta_{uij}}}
    = -\sigma(-\Delta_{uij}) =: -e_{uij},
    \label{eq:dldelta}
\end{equation}
where $e_{uij} \in (0, 1)$ is large when the pair is ranked wrongly and close
to zero when the positive product is far ahead. From \eqref{eq:score},
\[
    \Delta_{uij} = b_i - b_j + p_u^\top (q_i - q_j) + p_u^\top W (z_i - z_j),
\]
so $\Delta_{uij}$ depends on $p_u$, $q_i$, $q_j$, $W$, $b_i$ and $b_j$ only,
with
\[
    \frac{\partial \Delta}{\partial p_u} = v_i - v_j, \quad
    \frac{\partial \Delta}{\partial q_i} = p_u, \quad
    \frac{\partial \Delta}{\partial q_j} = -p_u, \quad
    \frac{\partial \Delta}{\partial W} = p_u (z_i - z_j)^\top, \quad
    \frac{\partial \Delta}{\partial b_i} = 1, \quad
    \frac{\partial \Delta}{\partial b_j} = -1 .
\]
The chain rule with \eqref{eq:dldelta} gives the gradients of one triplet:
\begin{align}
    \nabla_{p_u} \ell_{uij} &= -e_{uij}\,(v_i - v_j), \label{eq:gp}\\
    \nabla_{q_i} \ell_{uij} &= -e_{uij}\,p_u, &
    \nabla_{q_j} \ell_{uij} &= +e_{uij}\,p_u, \label{eq:gq}\\
    \nabla_{W} \ell_{uij} &= -e_{uij}\,p_u (z_i - z_j)^\top, \label{eq:gw}\\
    \frac{\partial \ell_{uij}}{\partial b_i} &= -e_{uij}, &
    \frac{\partial \ell_{uij}}{\partial b_j} &= +e_{uij}. \label{eq:gb}
\end{align}
All other parameters have zero gradient for this triplet. Each update moves
$p_u$ towards $v_i$ and away from $v_j$, moves $q_i$ towards $p_u$ and $q_j$
away from it, and raises $b_i$ while lowering $b_j$, in proportion to how
wrongly the pair is currently ranked.

\paragraph{Mini-batch and regularization.} The gradient of $L_B$ is the
average of \eqref{eq:gp}--\eqref{eq:gb} over the batch. A row of $P$, $Q$ or
$b$ accumulates one term for each triplet of the batch in which it appears,
and $W$ accumulates a term from every triplet. Adding the gradient of the
penalty in \eqref{eq:objective},
\begin{align}
    \nabla_{p_u} F_B &= -\frac{1}{|B|}\sum_{(u,i,j) \in B} e_{uij}\,(v_i - v_j)
        + \lambda_f\, p_u, \\
    \nabla_{q_m} F_B &= -\frac{1}{|B|}\Bigl(\sum_{(u,m,j) \in B} e_{umj}\,p_u
        - \sum_{(u,i,m) \in B} e_{uim}\,p_u\Bigr) + \lambda_f\, q_m, \\
    \frac{\partial F_B}{\partial b_m} &= -\frac{1}{|B|}\Bigl(\sum_{(u,m,j) \in B} e_{umj}
        - \sum_{(u,i,m) \in B} e_{uim}\Bigr) + \lambda_f\, b_m, \\
    \nabla_{W} F_B &= -\frac{1}{|B|}\sum_{(u,i,j) \in B} e_{uij}\,p_u (z_i - z_j)^\top
        + \lambda_w\, W .
\end{align}
The penalty gradient is applied to every learned parameter at every step, not
only to the rows that appear in the batch. Under uniform sampling of batches
this makes $\nabla F_B$ an unbiased estimate of $\nabla F$; shrinking only the
rows in the batch would instead weight each row's penalty by how often it is
sampled and so change the objective.

\paragraph{Implementation and verification.} The gradients are implemented
directly from these formulas with array operations and no automatic
differentiation. The term $e_{uij}$ is evaluated as
$\exp(-\log(1 + e^{\Delta_{uij}}))$ with a stable $\log(1 + e^x)$, so large
score differences cannot overflow. We verified the implementation against
central finite differences on 60 randomly chosen entries of each of $P$, $Q$,
$W$ and $b$, on a batch of 256 triplets and at parameters far from the
initial point; all agree to a relative tolerance of $10^{-5}$.

\subsection{Convexity Analysis}

\begin{proposition}[Joint non-convexity]
$F$ is not jointly convex in $(P, Q, W, b)$ in general.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The score difference contains the products $p_u^\top q_i$ and $p_u^\top W
z_i$ of two sets of variables, so it is bilinear, not affine, in $\theta$.
Consider the origin $\theta = 0$ and a direction $d = (\delta P, \delta Q,
\delta W, 0)$. Along $t d$ the biases stay at zero and
$\Delta_\tau(t d) = t^2 \kappa_\tau(d)$ with
$\kappa_\tau(d) = \delta p_u^\top\bigl(\delta q_i - \delta q_j
+ \delta W (z_i - z_j)\bigr)$.
Since $\log(1 + e^{-x}) = \log 2 - x/2 + O(x^2)$,
\begin{equation}
    F(t d) = \log 2 + t^2 \Bigl[ -\frac{1}{2N}\sum_{\tau \in \mathcal{T}} \kappa_\tau(d)
    + \frac{\lambda_f}{2}\bigl(\norm{\delta P}_F^2 + \norm{\delta Q}_F^2\bigr)
    + \frac{\lambda_w}{2}\norm{\delta W}_F^2 \Bigr] + O(t^4).
    \label{eq:curvature}
\end{equation}
Take a unit vector $x \in \R^k$, and set $\delta P = a x^\top$ and
$\delta Q = c x^\top$ with unit vectors $a \in \R^{170}$, $c \in \R^{206}$
and $\delta W = 0$. Then $\sum_\tau \kappa_\tau(d) = a^\top C c$, where
$C_{um}$ counts the triplets in which customer $u$ has product $m$ as the
positive item minus those in which it is the comparison item. Choosing $a$
and $c$ as the leading singular vectors of $C$, the bracket in
\eqref{eq:curvature} equals $\lambda_f - \sigma_{\max}(C)/(2N)$. The curvature
of $F$ along $d$ at the origin is therefore negative whenever
\begin{equation}
    \lambda_f < \frac{\sigma_{\max}(C)}{2N},
    \label{eq:threshold-f}
\end{equation}
and a function with negative curvature along a line is not convex.
\end{proof}

The same argument with $\delta P = a x^\top$ and $\delta W = x w^\top$ gives
negative curvature whenever
$\sqrt{\lambda_f \lambda_w} < \sigma_{\max}(D)/(2N)$, where row $u$ of $D$ is
$\sum_{(u,i,j) \in \mathcal{T}} (z_i - z_j)^\top$. For the supplied triplets,
$\sigma_{\max}(C) = 30.35$ and $\sigma_{\max}(D) = 50.29$, so $F$ is
non-convex at the origin whenever $\lambda_f < 2.79 \times 10^{-3}$ or
$\sqrt{\lambda_f \lambda_w} < 4.63 \times 10^{-3}$. We confirmed both
thresholds numerically with second differences of $F$ along these directions.
The origin matters in practice because the prescribed initial point is close
to it.

Two remarks qualify this result. First, the non-convexity comes from the model
and not from the loss: $\theta = (P, Q, W, b)$ and $(-P, -Q, -W, b)$ give the
same scores and the same value of $F$, while their midpoint $(0, 0, 0, b)$
has no personalization at all. Whenever personalization lowers the objective,
the midpoint is worse than both end points, which convexity forbids. Second,
the non-convexity is not unconditional. The Hessian of $L_{\mathcal{T}}$ is
bounded below by $-\sqrt{6}\, I$, because $|\partial \ell / \partial \Delta|
< 1$ and the constant Hessian of each $\Delta_\tau$ has spectral norm at most
$\sqrt{6}$ for unit-norm $z_i$. Hence $F$ is jointly convex when
$\min(\lambda_f, \lambda_w) \ge \sqrt{6}$, but such penalties are hundreds of
times larger than useful values and force all parameters towards zero.

\begin{proposition}[Block convexity]
For $\lambda_f, \lambda_w > 0$:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item with $(Q, W, b)$ fixed, $F$ is $\lambda_f$-strongly convex in $P$;
    \item with $P$ fixed, $F$ is strongly convex in $(Q, W, b)$ with modulus
    $\min(\lambda_f, \lambda_w)$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The function $x \mapsto \log(1 + e^{-x})$ is convex, since its second
derivative $\sigma(x)\sigma(-x)$ is positive.

(1) With $(Q, W, b)$ fixed, every $v_i$ and $b_i$ is a constant, and
$\Delta_{uij} = (v_i - v_j)^\top p_u + (b_i - b_j)$ is affine in $P$. A convex
function of an affine map is convex, and so is an average of convex functions,
so $L_{\mathcal{T}}$ is convex in $P$. Adding $\tfrac{\lambda_f}{2}\norm{P}_F^2$
makes $F$ $\lambda_f$-strongly convex in $P$; the remaining penalty terms are
constants.

(2) With $P$ fixed,
$\Delta_{uij} = b_i - b_j + p_u^\top q_i - p_u^\top q_j
+ \langle W, p_u (z_i - z_j)^\top \rangle$ is linear in $(Q, W, b)$. The same
argument shows that $L_{\mathcal{T}}$ is convex in $(Q, W, b)$, and the
penalty $\tfrac{\lambda_f}{2}(\norm{Q}_F^2 + \norm{b}_2^2)
+ \tfrac{\lambda_w}{2}\norm{W}_F^2$ makes $F$ strongly convex with modulus
$\min(\lambda_f, \lambda_w)$.
\end{proof}

In the first block the problem also separates across customers: each $p_u$
solves its own $k$-dimensional regularized logistic regression, with the
differences $v_i - v_j$ as features.

\paragraph{Role of positive regularization.} Without the penalties the block
problems are convex but can fail to have a unique minimizer, or any minimizer:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \emph{Flat directions.} Adding the same constant to every $b_i$
    leaves all $\Delta_{uij}$ unchanged, as does changing $W$ in any direction
    orthogonal to all matrices $p_u (z_i - z_j)^\top$, so minimizers are never
    unique.
    \item \emph{No minimizer.} If the comparisons of a block can all be ranked
    correctly, the logistic loss can be driven towards zero only by letting
    the parameters grow without bound, so the infimum is not attained.
\end{itemize}
With $\lambda_f, \lambda_w > 0$ each block problem is strongly convex. It has
exactly one minimizer, which is finite, and gradient-based block solvers
converge to it at a linear rate. This is what makes the alternating scheme of
BCD well defined: each block update is the approximate solution of a problem
with a unique answer, even though the joint problem is non-convex and the
alternation is only guaranteed to reach a stationary point.
